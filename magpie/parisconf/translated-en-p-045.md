Selectors: There are in the machine four pyramids of relays which each permit the choice among 64 alternatives and 125 small pyramids choosing between 2 alternatives. By passing one of the connections that comprise an instruction through one of these pyramids, one obtains that the address in question is subject to a choice, that is to say conditional.

Functioning. A chain of 840 relays (since January 1951, 1,200) is constructed in such a way that only one relay can be operated at a time. Each relay of the chain activates a particular instruction among those which are coupled on the panels. After execution of the indicated operation, the chain passes to the next instruction, normally from n to n + 1; by a coupling on the jump panel, this order can however be modified at will; a jump becomes conditional if one passes the coupling through a pyramid.

Manual operations. The machine can operate automatically; then the operations follow each other with the speeds indicated above, or manually, that is to say as slowly as one wishes. In manual operation, it is possible, with the aid of lamps on the control table, to follow what is happening in the machine during each instruction.

Practical information. Envisaged and projected by M. C. Palm who then directed the construction, the BARK was projected in detail by a small discussion group, drawn up for the most part by MM. Freese and Neovius, and built at the factories of the Royal Administration of Telegraphs and Telephones of Sweden.

The first plans were discussed in December 1948, and in February 1950 the machine accomplished its first work: a table of cosines.

The expenses have been approximately 400,000 Swedish crowns, that is to say around 25 million French francs.

The material used in the BARK is that which is ordinary in automatic telephony; very few things were constructed specially for this machine. In particular, the methods of assembly and coupling are identical to those used in a telephone exchange. Naturally, this fact has contributed greatly to diminishing construction time.

We shall give examples which will illustrate on one hand some methods of "checking" which have proved useful for the BARK, on the other hand the possibilities of manual intervention which this machine offers.
