Thus the slow and constant accommodation of the eye which achieves an average illumination, the pupillary response which diminishes the quantity of light falling on the retina so as to maintain quasi-constant the total number of excited cones and rods, and finally the rapid-action "negative feedbacks" in the brain itself, make it possible for us to see the same figure in the faint light of the stars and in full sunlight. A second reflex, passing from the eye through the mesencephalon to the control centres for eye movements, calculates by how much and in what direction the eye must be turned to centre an object appearing in our visual field. The result is sent to the eye muscles which then turn the eye so as to reduce this vector to zero. This achieves the centring of the form to be seen; from that moment on, it becomes indifferent in what part of the visual field it first appeared to us. During the entire time of the rapid eye movement, impulses coming from the retina to the visual cortex produce only a "flickering" of signals. As soon as the form is centred, the size of the figure no longer matters, and the visual cortex itself [produces] its outputs so as to provide the form regardless of size. All this has been presented in detail by PITTS and myself in previous publications.

The process of calculating form without regard to size is achieved through cycles, each of them taking roughly one-tenth of a second; these cycles correspond to the alpha waves of the brain, their frequency being moreover divisible by two or doubling through suitable sensory training. Unless I am mistaken, this frequency was first reported as characteristic of cortical activities by BABINSKI, but a real understanding of its importance and its precise measurement in sensory physiology was accomplished only by CRAIK and STROUD. For many of us, the most reasonable supposition seems to be that in the visual cortex, by virtue of an oblique ascent and a vertical descent of information through successive mosaics of relays, by virtue of a scanning wave that alerts one layer after another, thus creating enlarged and diminished images of retinal inputs that will finally be conveyed in vertically descending fibres to be summed in other regions of the brain. Each of these scans corresponds to a snapshot of the external world, and because of this our experience undergoes a new quantification at this level. Movements, except for those along the line of sight, are seen as differences between successive snapshots, and with the displacement of recognizable forms the perception of "cause and effect" is introduced.

Man remembers many of these snapshots in surprisingly detailed fashion, and for this he must use "engrams" (fixed traces) in replacement of reverberations.
