Inductive reasoning

Consider a finite set of objects; each of them is described by a group of digits, each of these digits defining the presence or absence of a property. It may be possible to observe a group of properties possessed by each of the objects; one can then establish a general law applying to the finite group considered. The discovery of such a law will be called an act of inductive reasoning. Any assertion that such a law can apply to hitherto unobserved facts is an act of scientific faith.

There is an important exception concerning inductive reasoning in the field of pure mathematics, when compared to that of the experimental sciences. In mathematics, the extrapolation of a law derived from observed objects to other as yet unobserved objects is scientifically and logically possible. Indeed, the objects in question - numbers - were initially defined according to their laws. All mathematical laws are merely modifications of these basic laws; and consequently extrapolation is possible. Whether the complex laws thus deduced are applicable to the physical world is not the concern of the pure mathematician.

It will be shown that inductive reasoning as described here is possible for a calculating machine. As an example, that of pattern recognition will be described. It will be shown that many intelligence test forms are possible for a machine. It will also be deduced that in the field of human and animal experimental psychology, one can decompose test sets into elements of memory and comparison, and thereby evaluate them quantitatively.
