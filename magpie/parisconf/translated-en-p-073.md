## Translation from French

- 6 -

a sort of statistical equilibrium between, on one hand, our input channels (approximately one million), each with a possible input at each millisecond, and on the other hand, a memory for "bits" each having a half-life of half a day; we thus find that the "bits" we could remember are far more numerous than the total number of existing relays (that is, 10^10 instead of 10^10). This leads us to suspect that the ultimate traces left are of an order of magnitude smaller than the neuron (for example, molecular structures), or must be sought in changes imprinted by use on the numerous and multiple neuronal interconnections.

Human memory differs from machines by the existence of numerous pathways for inputs and reminiscences; it follows that instead of having to examine one after another all positions to retrieve a snapshot, we can immediately access all snapshots whose content relates to the question under consideration.

There is another advantage enjoyed by the brain worth mentioning. The maximum number of outputs is on the order of one decision per millisecond, but our numerous input pathways permit us to base this decision on a hundred million "bits" per millisecond, and this for our eyes alone. We obtain greater security by requiring agreement before information is passed to the next stage. This requires only that neurons detect coincidences, as noted by ROSENBLUETH and WIENER regarding the rhythmic contraction appearing in stretched muscles, contractions called clonic: in this case the value of the nerve impulse frequency appears in the form of a sinusoidal function in the exponent.

This brings us to the question of how information is encoded in the nervous system. In calculating machines we achieve compression of numbers through a coding in which the position of a "bit" is the exponent of its root. The nervous system uses similar logarithmic procedures. Thus, the frequency of impulses from any receptor is proportional to the logarithm of the stimulation intensity. Sound pitch is mapped onto the cerebral cortex such that the interval of different octaves corresponds to the same length on the cortex; vision of a point likewise projects so that the radian distance along the cortex between its cortical projection and the projection of the midpoint of the visual field is proportional to the logarithm of the angle of the lines of sight. In each of these cases, logarithmic encoding permits just noticeable differences that are proportional to the quantities to be recorded.

Generally speaking, since there exists a largely sufficient number of neurons and since the cross-sectional area of their axons is small, the essential mode of encoding uses positions; the body surface with its sensory receptors is topologically mapped at the level of the cerebellum as well as the thalamus and cerebral cortex. The same applies to commands of the musculature controlled by central organs.

- [Note: The original text has "10^10 au lieu de 10^10" which appears to be a transcription error - the second number was likely different, possibly 10^12, to make the comparison meaningful]

- [Translation note: McCulloch's observation about logarithmic encoding in the nervous system relates to Weber-Fechner law, a foundational principle of psychophysics established in the 19th century]
- [Translation note: The comparison of memory capacity to "bits" and discussion of encoding reflects the influence of Shannon's newly published information theory (1948)]
