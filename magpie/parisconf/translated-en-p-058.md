W. Grey WALTER


MECHANICAL REALIZATION OF MODELS OF CEREBRAL STRUCTURE

Presentation of artificial animals


Physiologists are often discouraged by the enormous complexity of the nervous system. The methods of study are, by far, out of proportion with the possible number of active units - more than ten billion in the case of the human brain. The subjective impressions of personality and the diversity of human experience seem to require a brain of great complication but theoretical considerations and experience suggest that elaboration may be, less in the number of units than in the richness of their interconnection or only in the latter. With only two active elements, seven modes of existence are possible (0, A, B, AB, A-bar B, AB-bar, A-bar B-bar); six elements would suffice to provide a new experience every tenth of a second during an entire long life. The brain may contain up to 1,000 active elements in the form of groups of similar neurones but experiments made on models containing only two elements confirm that this behaviour, apparently unpredictable, capable of purpose and discernment, can result from permutation operations proper to them. These models (which we hope to present) illustrate two of the basic principles of the animal type - ECONOMY and PLASTICITY. They manifest the following traits of behaviour.


1. Searching (scanning). In the absence of an adequate excitation (signal) such as a light, the model is in constant movement and searches its horizon until it receives a signal or its motive power is exhausted.

2. Positive tropism (oriented feedback). When an adequate light signal is received, the searching process is stopped and the direction servo-motor leads the model toward the light source.

3. Seeking of an optimum. When the intensity of the light source exceeds a certain value, the direction servo-motor is put back into service so that the model avoids the source and turns around it.

## Notes
- [Translation note: W. Grey Walter (1910-1977) was a British neurophysiologist famous for his "tortoises" - Elmer and Elsie (1948-49) - simple electromechanical robots that exhibited surprisingly complex behaviors from minimal components. His work demonstrated that apparent intelligence could emerge from simple circuits, profoundly influencing robotics and AI. "Buridan's dilemma" refers to the medieval paradox of a donkey starving between two equidistant hay bales - Walter's robots resolved this through their scanning mechanism.]
