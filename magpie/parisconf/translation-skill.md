## Translation Guidelines

### Principle 1: Preserve Period Voice
Do NOT modernize terminology when it would lose meaning. A 1951 scientist saying "machine à calculer" was not saying "computer" - they were using the dominant terminology of their era.

**Approach:**
- Primary translation: Use period-appropriate English
- Annotation: [Modern: ...] where helpful
- Context: Explain what the concept became

**Example:**
> "Les machines à calculer peuvent-elles penser?"

**Translation:**
> "Can calculating machines think?" [Modern: Can computers think? - anticipating Turing's question]

### Principle 2: Recognize Pre-Disciplinary Concepts

Many passages describe concepts that would later get names. Flag these:

**Example:**
> "régions associatives d'un cerveau... dispersion harmonieuse des informations"

**Translation:**
> "associative regions of the brain... harmonious dispersion of information"
> [Note: This describes what would later be called "distributed processing" in neural networks]

### Principle 3: Handle Mixed Language

The proceedings mix French and English. Some talks were given in English, some in French. Preserve this:

- French abstracts → Translate to English
- English abstracts → Keep as-is, note [Original in English]
- Technical terms that crossed languages → Note the borrowing

### Principle 4: Explain the Question Being Asked

1951 scientists were asking questions that now seem obvious or naive. Contextualize:

- "Can machines think?" was genuinely open
- The brain/computer analogy was new and exciting
- Cybernetics promised unification of mind and machine
- These were SERIOUS scientists, not dreamers

### Principle 5: Technical Accuracy for Period Hardware

When describing machines, preserve technical specificity:
- "250 tubes" = 250 vacuum tubes (logic elements)
- "256 mots à 6 décimales" = 256 words of 6 decimal digits each
- "tambour magnétique" = magnetic drum storage

## Historical Significance

This conference represents:

1. **The moment before AI** - Scientists asking "can machines think?" without a field to answer it
2. **International collaboration** - Post-WWII scientific cooperation
3. **Hardware diversity** - Before standardization, many approaches competed
4. **The cybernetic moment** - When brain/machine analogies seemed most promising
5. **French computing history** - Institut Blaise Pascal's role

The proceedings are a time capsule of how brilliant scientists thought about minds and machines before the concepts we now take for granted were invented.
