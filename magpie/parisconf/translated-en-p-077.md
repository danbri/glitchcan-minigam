According to potential theory, we know that the nerve placed in salt water will at the moment of its excitation cause electrical currents in the medium external to the nerve, currents whose intensity is proportional to the second derivative of the above monophasic curve; an electrode situated in this external conducting medium will pick up a potential variation with a triphasic appearance: positive - negative - positive. This current, like any other, will affect any nerve placed in the vicinity by raising then lowering, then raising again its excitation threshold. Such curves of excitability variation have been drawn by MARRAZZI and LORENTE DE NO and by ROSENBLUETH.

Let us now examine what will happen when the impulse with its triphasic action approaches a block and dies there. Just a little before the block we will have first an inhibition, thus an increase in threshold, and then an important and long period of facilitation: indeed, in this region the recorded potential is mainly convex upward. By contrast, at the level of the block and beyond, where the recorded potential is only concave upward during the entire time it fades there, we will have only an inhibition. On the right of the figure will be found two examples of the variation of this second derivative and the threshold variations caused at two points of the nerve.

If finally we create, in the test nerve, a region where impulse conduction is difficult, a region we shall call the decrement region, the success or failure for impulses to pass at this place will depend on the electrical conditions encountered there. We will carry out the experiment by placing such a decremental region of the test nerve along the conditioning nerve and stimulating it at one of its extremities and recording at the other. The experiment, stimulating the test nerve at different moments relative to the time of arrival and disappearance of impulses at the block level of the conditioning nerve, shows that the neighborhood action exerted under these conditions by the conditioning stimulus is truly powerful. We think these results could apply to the fate of afferent muscle impulses approaching the motoneuron region, since every decrease in the diameter of the axon or its terminals, decrease inevitable at each bifurcation of fine fibers, will make conduction more hazardous. And thus impulses in these regions are at the mercy of relatively weak currents, currents due to impulses of different origin but traveling toward the same cells.

It is evident from this that impulses brought by fine fibers from the ventral part of the spinal cord and which have an inhibitory action will create conditions making difficult the transmission of excitations coming through the dorsal roots.
