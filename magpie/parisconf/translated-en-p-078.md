## Translation from French

- 11 -

In the bushy arborization, an impulse in any branch will tend to facilitate a contemporary impulse from a neighboring branch, an impulse that otherwise, for the reasons invoked above, might perhaps have died. But in the tree with sparse branches, the impulse in one branch is of little help for the impulse of a neighboring branch, and the tendency for an impulse to die before the anatomical termination of the arborization may be greater. Now suppose two such arborizations intermingled, one sparse, the other dense, their terminals approaching a cell body through its dendritic tree. If impulses travel in these two arborizations simultaneously, what will happen will be very similar to what would happen in the dense arborization alone. By contrast, if impulses in the sparse arborization precede impulses from the dense arborization and die before reaching the motoneuron, they will inhibit - I would prefer to say arrest - the impulses coming through the dense tree in the region where they themselves died. Due to the sparse distribution of impulses, minimal deflections will be recorded in the vicinity of the motoneuron body and no deflection propagated electrotonically in the anterior root. This is what the experiment shows. If one places a microelectrode somewhere in the vicinity of the cell body and tries to combine the effects of an inhibitory volley and a test volley, there is no reason to expect an appreciable reduction of the presynaptic potential. Generally, none is indeed recorded. In this region, the spike corresponding to the discharge of a cell will be recorded as a positive deflection; we are indeed closer to the dendrites than to the cell body. But if the microelectrode is practically right against the cell body, the spike is recorded in the form of a negativity, and here inhibition will diminish the presynaptic potential.

If we succeed in stimulating few excitatory fibers and thus obtain that the impulses die before arriving at the soma of the motoneuron, this first volley can be inhibitory for the following one arriving by the same pathway. We have been able to demonstrate this.

Let us consider what will happen if we intermingle two systems of dense arborizations. Each will tend to facilitate what happens in the other maximally when their stimulation was simultaneous; this reciprocal facilitation will be less if the respective stimulations are separated by time intervals, and facilitation decreases exponentially as a function of the temporal gap between the two stimulations. It is very similar for inhibition. This is a way of quickly expressing what DAVID LLOYD has found for the time course of monosynaptic facilitation and inhibition in the spinal cord.

I have described all this at some length so that you will not think we need in the brain relay systems whose interaction would have something mysterious about it; but it should not be thought that the whole story is there.
