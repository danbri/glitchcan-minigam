- II -

I would like to devote the second part of my presentation to the description of more elementary mechanisms relating to the way neurons excite and inhibit each other.

Figure 1 represents a neuron as a cell having a body and ramifications called dendrites, and a long thin root, the axon, which divides into increasingly fine filaments to end in the vicinity of other neurons or at the level of a muscle. By burning sugars and oxygen, a potential difference is established between the positively charged exterior and the negatively charged interior of the neuron; if its exterior becomes negative at any given place, the electrical resistance in that region decreases abruptly and that place becomes a "sink" making neighboring parts appear as "sources"; an electrical current will thus be established; soon the voltage reduction that results in neighboring parts will compel them to become sinks themselves, relative to more distant parts of the neuron. Thus will appear the action potential that will propagate from its initial point in both opposite directions along the nerve like a "smoke ring" of local currents; it can, however, under certain conditions be stopped at any place by a positive-direction change in the cellular electrical potential.

A neuron can thus be activated at any point along its path and present along its entire path an impulse whose magnitude depends solely on local cellular conditions. You see here such a cell and its branches drawn by CAJAL. This is the type called a motoneuron, whose axon (A) will exit the spinal cord through its ventral root and go to a muscle.

Figure 2: In the two-neuron reflex arc responsible for the stretch reflex, the motoneurons are excited by impulses born of the stretch itself. These impulses travel in neurons going from the muscle through the posterior spinal roots to the motoneuron; the terminals of these afferent neurons approach the cell body of the motoneuron by forcing a passage through its dendrites and terminate right against this cell body, constituting what have been called "terminal buttons."

This figure is by CAJAL. The impulses coming thus to the body of the motoneuron die there and create a "sink," while a current "source" will appear in the dendritic zone. Thus impulses will be born in the body of the motoneuron that will soon invade its axon to go to the muscle, but cannot go back up into its dendrites.

## Translation Notes

- CAJAL - Santiago Ramon y Cajal (1852-1934), Spanish neuroscientist, Nobel Prize 1906, founder of modern neuroscience
- "boutons terminaux" - terminal buttons (synaptic boutons), the bulb-like endings of axons
- "motoneurone" - motoneuron (motor neuron), a nerve cell that controls muscle fibers
- [Translation note: McCulloch is presenting fundamental neurophysiology to explain how the "relays" of the nervous system actually work at the cellular level]
- The "smoke ring" analogy for action potential propagation is a vivid description of the wave of depolarization traveling along the axon
