# Mystery 3: three premises with a new protagonist (gate 1, for the owner)

Status: draft, October 2026. Nothing is made yet. Choose one premise, or parts of two. Then I write the case file
(the crime minute by minute, every lie, every clue and where the reader finds it) and a text-only playable draft
(gate 2) before any pictures or voices.

All three are set in the same valley as Steeple Wyke, in the same October. Each new protagonist works alone at first.
Each premise has a planned point where the protagonist meets DCI Quaile and DS Sam Adeyemi, and the reader can see
the same events from the other side.

What all three add, from the "take it up a notch" list:

- The reader plays the new protagonist ("you"), as Sam before.
- Two real systems that the reader can check, as with change ringing and Gill Sans in chapter 2. Each system is
  shown on the page as real text (a log, a table, a photograph with a scale), not as small print in a picture.
- Statements that the reader can disprove: in an interview the reader presents an item from the notebook against a
  statement. A wrong item costs trust, and that person tells you less.
- A case-board page where the reader joins two facts into a conclusion. Each conclusion opens new questions.
- At least two wrong answers that the evidence partly supports. Each wrong suspect has a real secret, so the lie
  that makes them look guilty has a reason.
- A limit on time, so some choices close other routes, and a second reading is different.
- An ending in three parts: who, how and why. Each wrong combination has its own ending.

## A. The coroner's officer: "Lock Fourteen"

**Protagonist.** Ros Kettering, 58, coroner's officer for the county: a civilian who investigates sudden deaths for
the coroner. Twenty years a police constable before that. She cannot arrest anyone, cannot make anyone talk, and
most people think she is there to do the paperwork. She retires in March and wants no open files when she goes.
She knows the procedure better than the police do and uses it.

**Setting.** The Wyke Arm, a canal branch that volunteers are bringing back into use, two miles below Steeple Wyke.
A flight of locks, a trust office in an old lock-keeper's cottage, hire boats.

**The death.** At dawn a volunteer finds Neville Strand, 66, chairman of the restoration trust, drowned in lock 14.
He walked the towpath every night. The first view: he fell in, in the dark. The police see no crime.

**What happened.** The trust paid £180,000 of grant money for two pairs of new oak gates for locks 13 and 14. The
treasurer, Clive Amory, bought reclaimed gates for £22,000 and painted them. He used the difference to replace the
trust's reserve, which he had lost on an investment two years before and hidden. He kept nothing for himself.
Neville found out, and at 23:15 met Clive at lock 14 to tell him so. They argued; Neville stepped back and fell
into the empty lock, onto the cill, and broke his leg. He was alive, in the water at the bottom of the chamber.
Clive opened the top paddles and filled the lock. Then he went home.

**The two systems.**

1. *The lock.* A boat that goes down through a lock leaves it empty; a boat that goes up leaves it full. The last
   boat, a hire boat, went down through 15 and 14 at 22:40 and left both empty (its crew's photographs show the
   time). At dawn lock 14 was full and lock 15 was still empty. No boat came up, because it would have had to go
   through 15 next and fill it. So a person filled lock 14 by hand, alone, after 22:40, with Neville in it. The
   post-mortem says he drowned: the water killed him, not the fall.
2. *The gates.* New gates carry a cast plate with the maker and the year. The gates on lock 14 have a plate that
   says 1987 under the new paint, and plugged bolt holes from an earlier lock. Neville's last photographs, on his
   phone, are of that plate.

**Suspects.**

- Clive Amory, 61, treasurer. Kind, careful, and the man who explains the lock system to you on page 1. Lies about
  the time he went to bed.
- The hire-boat crew, Joost and Maaike van Dam, a Dutch couple in their thirties. Neville shouted at them at lock
  15 about leaving a paddle up, and they were the last people to see him. They lie that they did not see him again,
  because at 23:00 they moored where mooring is not allowed and walked back to the pub.
- Kit Rowe, 24, the only paid lock-keeper. She has a key to the paddle locks and a strong motive: Neville had
  written to the trust to end her post. She lies about where she was, because she was at a job interview in Bristol
  that she does not want the trust to know about.

**The reader's work.** Ros has two weeks before the inquest opens. She chooses who she asks, what she puts in the
file and which witnesses the coroner calls. A coroner's conclusion can be "accident", "open" or "unlawful
killing", and it may not name a person. So to name a killer, Ros must take the file to the police. That is the
meeting with Quaile, on the last page: the reader decides what to give her and whom to name.

**Theme.** Levels: water finds its level, and so do accounts. Clive's crime starts as an attempt to put a hidden
loss right.

**Why it could fail.** The lock logic must be shown so clearly that a reader who has never seen a canal can follow
it in one page. Page 1 does it as Clive's tour of the lock for the new coroner's officer, which also puts the
killer at your side from the start.

## B. The district nurse: "The Round"

**Protagonist.** Lin Hartigan, 39, community nurse for the valley. Eleven patients a day in a car that will not
take Wyke Hill in second gear. She wants her patients to stay in their own homes, and she knows the inside of
every kitchen in the valley. She has things she may not tell anyone: what a patient tells her is confidential.

**The death.** Edwin Marsh, 84, a retired surveyor with diabetes and bad arthritis, is found dead in his bed. His
GP gives the cause as low blood sugar in a diabetic man of 84, and signs the certificate. The cremation is in four
days.

**What happened.** Edwin had lent £14,000 to his neighbour, Roger Penhale, 70, a retired pharmacist, and had
asked his nephew to collect it. Roger is the emergency contact for Edwin's key safe. At 02:04 he let himself in,
dialled 40 units on Edwin's insulin pen (Edwin's dose was 12, each morning) and injected him as he slept. A
pharmacist knows that this looks like a natural death in a man like Edwin.

**The two systems.**

1. *The pen.* Edwin's smart insulin pen records the time and the size of each dose. The last entry is 40 units at
   02:10. Edwin's hands could not turn the dial that far; the carers set his morning dose for him.
2. *The key safe and the carers' log.* The key safe gives each holder a separate code and records each opening.
   Agency carers log each visit with an app that records the time and place. The 02:04 opening used the emergency
   code. No carer logged a visit after 21:30.

**Suspects.**

- Grant Marsh, 52, the nephew, who has power of attorney and wants to sell the house. He lies that he was not in
  the county that week, because he was looking at a care home for Edwin, which Edwin had refused.
- Kelly Brand, 31, the agency carer. Her app shows a visit at 21:30 that she did not make: she logged it from her
  car to keep her hours. She lies to keep her job.
- Roger Penhale, the neighbour, who found the body in the morning and phoned Lin first.

**The reader's work.** Lin cannot stop her round. Each day has six visits at fixed times, and her patients are her
witnesses, so the reader chooses which visits become questions and which patients get less of her time. A missed
patient has a cost, which the story remembers. Some patients tell her things in confidence: the reader decides
whether to break that, with consequences. The four days to the cremation are the limit: the ending depends on
whether Lin persuades the coroner's office to stop it, and what she can prove.

**Theme.** Care, and the people who have keys to your house.

**Crossover.** Win Haskett (chapter 2) is on Lin's round, for her ears. Lin and Quaile meet in Win's front room.

**Why it could fail.** The death is close to real cases, and a nurse as detective must not make medicine look
easy or careless. It needs a light touch on the medical facts, and Edwin needs to be a person before he is a body:
page 1 is Lin's last visit to him, the day before.

## C. The loss adjuster: "Proof of Ownership"

**Protagonist.** Declan Roe, 47, a freelance loss adjuster from Cork, paid by insurers to check claims. He is
honest and believes nobody, and everyone he meets wants money from him. He is good at prices and bad at people.

**The fire.** A thatched house at Wyke Mill burns down at night. The owner, Felicity Vane, claims £2.3 million,
most of it for a painting. Her lodger, a young bell-hanger working on St Aldhelm's, died in the fire.

**What happened.** Two crimes, by two people. In December Felicity sold the painting privately, through Jeremy Cole
(chapter 2), to pay her debts, and kept the insurance. In October her son Hugo set the fire to end the lodger's
tenancy, so that he could sell the house; he believed the lodger was away for the week. The lodger had come back
early. Felicity did not know about the fire; Hugo did not know about the painting.

**The two systems.**

1. *Shadows.* Felicity's "proof of ownership" photograph shows the painting on the wall, with sunlight from the
   window across it, and she says she took it in May. The angle of the shadow of the window bar fits only the low
   sun of December, after the sale.
2. *The smart meter.* The house meter records the electricity used each half hour. Felicity says the house was
   empty from six. The meter shows the lodger's electric shower at 01:30, and nothing from the heater that she says
   started the fire.

**Suspects.** Felicity (the fraud, not the fire), Hugo (the fire), and the thatcher who rethatched the roof in
September and cut corners on the fire barrier: a fair wrong answer for the fire.

**The reader's work.** Declan offers money. Every interview is a negotiation, and a payment offered too early
closes a line of questions. The ending separates the two crimes: a reader who names one culprit for both is wrong.

**Crossover.** Jeremy Cole, on remand after chapter 2, is a witness: Declan has to visit him, and Quaile has to
allow it.

**Why it could fail.** Two crimes are harder to keep fair than one, and a death in a fire is grim for a cosy
story. It needs the most care of the three.

## My recommendation

**A, "Lock Fourteen".** Reasons:

- The inquest gives the three-part ending without extra machinery: the conclusion, what goes in the file, and
  whom Ros names to the police.
- A protagonist with no powers makes every interview a choice with a cost. That is where the depth comes from.
- The lock logic is a clean system that the reader can check, as change ringing was in chapter 2.
- The meeting with Quaile is part of the plot, not added. Later chapters can show the same events from both sides.
- Water, locks and towpaths suit the moving panels that worked best: fire, water and wind.

B is the strongest on character and the simplest to keep fair. C is the most ambitious and the most likely to go
wrong.
